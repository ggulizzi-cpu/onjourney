Global Style Prefix (applied to every prompt) — HIS EXCELLENCY house style 
Style: Ultra-photorealistic cinematic mockumentary — The Office (US) documentary grammar set in July 1775. Shot on 35mm film, handheld documentary camera. No 3D render, no game engine, no stylization.
World: Continental Army camp, Cambridge, Massachusetts, summer 1775. Everything period-authentic — homespun linen, mud, canvas tents, muskets, goats. Absolutely no modern objects, no anachronisms.
Lighting: Natural light only — overcast New England daylight in exteriors; window and candle light in interiors. Camera on the shadow side. Faint campfire-smoke haze in exteriors.
Camera: Documentary-crew behavior — handheld micro-shake, snap-zooms onto reactions, refocus pulls, eavesdropping long lens from between tents. Every camera move is motivated: the operator is hunting the joke.
Acting: Dry deadpan workplace-documentary delivery. Micro-pauses before reactions, precise eye-lines, living eyes with catch-lights, chest rise from breathing. Nobody mugs — the comedy is restraint. Characters may glance at the lens; the crew is never acknowledged in dialogue.

Chain of command — Washington discovers that in this army, authority flows upward from Ezekiel.

Characters:
GENERAL WASHINGTON — matches the first reference image exactly: mid-40s, tall, broad, auburn hair tied back in a queue, immaculate blue-and-buff Continental general's uniform, white gloves held in his left hand. Two days into command; his patience has thinned but his posture has not.
SERGEANT — New England militiaman, late 30s, sunburnt, patchy brown wool coat with mismatched buttons, three-day beard, the relaxed confidence of an elected official.
EZEKIEL — lanky private, early 20s, homespun linen shirt, hat tipped over his eyes, spectacularly at ease.

Scene:
Mid-morning, overcast. A muddy work area between tent rows: a half-dug trench with two shovels standing in it, untouched. Ezekiel lounges against a split-rail fence at frame right, ten feet from the trench. Washington stands frame-left facing the Sergeant frame-right, eight feet apart, the trench between them. Camp bustle continues behind them, indifferent.

CUT 1 — Handheld 35mm two-shot, chest height, slow lateral drift right, Washington frame-left, Sergeant frame-right:
Washington's eyes travel from the empty trench to the lounging private — an unhurried, complete assessment — then back to the Sergeant. A measured beat. Evenly, quietly: "Sergeant. Order that man to dig." His chin indicates Ezekiel with the smallest possible movement. The Sergeant follows the gesture, then looks back, already apologetic.

CUT 2 — Snap-zoom past their shoulders to Ezekiel at the fence, long-lens 85mm feel, quick refocus:
Ezekiel, eyes closed under his tipped hat, arms folded, one boot crossed over the other, profoundly at peace. He exhales contentedly. Hold the comic beat a full two seconds. A goat bleats somewhere off-screen. Nobody speaks.

CUT 3 — Reverse to the Sergeant, 50mm medium, static, Washington's shoulder soft in the left foreground:
The Sergeant leans in half a step, confidential, with the warm, patient tone of a man explaining something obvious to a foreigner: "Can't, sir. Ezekiel voted for me." A small, genuinely sympathetic shrug.

CUT 4 — 85mm close on Washington, static, shallow focus, overcast key from his right:
A micro-pause. His jaw sets a fraction. Flat, without heat: "That is not how armies work." From off-screen, the Sergeant's voice, cheerfully agreeing: "It's how this one works." Washington blinks once, and his eyes travel slowly — deliberately — to the documentary lens and hold there as his chest rises with one long, controlled breath.

General Charles Lee arrives — dogs first — and opens his campaign for Washington's job.

Characters:
GENERAL WASHINGTON — matches the first reference image exactly: mid-40s, tall, auburn hair tied back, immaculate blue-and-buff uniform, gloves in left hand. He carries the quiet defeat of the Ezekiel conversation from the previous scene — composed, but his politeness has become armor.
GENERAL CHARLES LEE — matches the second reference image exactly: mid-40s, gaunt, hawk-nosed, wind-blown dark hair, a worn campaign coat with faded scarlet facings, mud-spattered boots with spurs. Brilliant, feral, certain he should have the job.
THREE DOGS — descending in size: a grey wolfhound, a liver-and-white setter, a small rough terrier. Muddy, confident, devoted to Lee.

Scene:
A camp lane between officer tents, minutes after the trench conversation. Same overcast light, same mud. Washington stands frame-left mid-lane, having just turned at the sound of barking. Lee approaches from frame-right, twenty feet out, his three dogs trotting ahead of him in loose formation. Soldiers step out of the dogs' way; nobody steps out of Lee's.

CUT 1 — Low tracking shot at dog height, wide 24mm, moving right-to-left with the dogs, mud filling the bottom of frame:
The three dogs trot through the lane with total authority, tails high, the terrier struggling to keep pace and refusing to admit it. Soldiers' boots shuffle aside at the edges of frame. At the end of the track the camera tilts up from the wolfhound to find LEE mid-stride behind his dogs, surveying the camp like an inspector who has already found it wanting.

CUT 2 — Handheld 35mm two-shot: Lee arrives frame-right and stops six feet from Washington frame-left, dogs settling in orbit around Lee's boots:
Lee looks Washington up and down — the uniform, the gloves, the posture — and relishes every syllable: "Washington. Or should I say — His Excellency." Then, turning his head to look directly into the documentary lens, conversational, as if the camera were an old friend: "I was in actual wars. European ones."

CUT 3 — Static 50mm two-shot, eye-level, locked off; the wolfhound sits down squarely between the two generals like a referee:
A beat of silence. Washington's eyes go down to the three dogs, one by one, then back up to Lee. Perfectly level, perfectly polite: "General Lee. Whose dogs are these?" The terrier scratches its ear. Lee's answer will have to wait — hold on the standoff, both men breathing, neither blinking first.
